Big idea

Treat each title as a feature vector, treat your taste as a user vector, and then measure how well they match.

title score = xi · u    and    similarity = (xi · u)/(‖xi‖‖u‖)
Interpretation: dot product rewards overlap in the same genres. Cosine similarity asks: “Do the title and the user point in the same taste direction?”

Step 5 — Rank everything

For every row of X, compute a score. Then sort from highest to lowest.

X ∈ ℝ8×6,   u ∈ ℝ6,   a = X u
Scoring rule: final = 60% normalized dot product + 40% cosine similarity. This keeps both strength and direction in the decision.

Step 6 — Your recommendations

The top titles become the final recommendations. You can present these as “Because you like sci‑fi and mystery...” or “Because your taste vector aligns strongly with these content vectors.”

What you just discovered

  • A user’s taste can be represented as a vector.
  • Each title can also be represented as a feature vector.
  • Dot product and cosine similarity help score how strongly a title aligns with a user’s preferences.

Why this matters

  • Netflix, YouTube, Spotify and e-commerce
  • Personalized feeds and suggestions
  • Recommender systems in AI products
Quick check

Test your understanding

What does the feature matrix store?

Rows for titles and columns for genre or content features.

Why use cosine similarity?

It measures how well the pattern of preferences aligns, not just the total magnitude.

True or false: the highest raw dot product always gives the fairest recommendation.

False — similarity in direction also matters, especially when magnitudes differ.

Common mistakes students make

  • Thinking recommendation is random or purely opinion-based.
  • Ignoring that two users with different magnitudes can still have similar taste directions.
  • Forgetting that a score is built from several mathematical signals, not one number alone.
